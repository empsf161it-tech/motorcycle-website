The AI Imperative: Why Every Enterprise Strategy Needs a Generative AI Chapter in 2025

Dr. Priya Nair
Managing Director, Digital Transformation
September 5, 2024  ·  12 min read

Generative AI has crossed the threshold from experiment to enterprise imperative. Companies that treat it as a tool have already fallen behind companies that treat it as a platform.

In the past 18 months, we have spoken with over 200 C-suite executives across 14 industries about their organizations' relationship with generative AI. The pattern is striking: almost every company has experiments running. Very few have a strategy. And the gap between those two states is measured in billions of dollars of competitive disadvantage.

This brief draws on those conversations and Apex's analysis of 60+ enterprise AI programs to outline the five moves that distinguish the companies building durable AI-enabled advantage from those accumulating a growing inventory of impressive demos that never scale.

Move 1: Reframe AI as an Operating Model Decision, Not a Technology Decision

The single most common mistake we see is placing AI ownership in the technology function. Generative AI at enterprise scale requires decisions about which tasks humans perform, which AI performs, and which require a new human-AI collaboration model. Those are operating model decisions, and they belong with the COO and CHRO as much as with the CTO.

The companies leading in enterprise AI adoption have established cross-functional AI governance bodies with real authority. They have redesigned roles, not just augmented them. They measure AI ROI in business outcomes—revenue per employee, resolution rate, time-to-insight—not in model performance metrics.

"We stopped asking 'how do we add AI to what we do?' and started asking 'what would we build from scratch if we could?' That reframe changed everything."
— CDO, Global Insurance Group

Move 2: Build a Proprietary Data Advantage Before It's Too Late

The foundational models from OpenAI, Google, Anthropic, and Meta are converging in capability. The companies that will win are those whose AI systems have access to proprietary data those models have never seen—patient records, engineering logs, customer interaction histories, financial transaction patterns.

Building a proprietary data advantage requires three investments most companies have deferred: a modern data architecture that makes proprietary data accessible for AI use cases, a data governance framework that protects privacy and quality without creating bureaucratic bottlenecks, and a culture of data contribution—where business units actively instrument their processes to generate high-quality training signal.

Move 3: Sequence Deployments for Compounding Returns

Most enterprise AI programs make deployment decisions based on feasibility and enthusiasm. The leaders make them based on sequencing logic—asking which use cases generate data and organizational learning that makes the next use case faster and better.

In financial services, for example, a well-sequenced approach might start with document intelligence (high feasibility, generates labeled data), progress to analyst augmentation (leverages the document intelligence data), and move toward automated advisory (combines analyst patterns with market data). Each wave generates the inputs that accelerate the next.

Move 4: Treat Change Management as a First-Class Deliverable

Our analysis of AI program failures identifies people resistance—not technology failure—as the primary cause in 68% of cases. This is entirely predictable and almost entirely preventable.

Enterprise AI deployments that succeed invest in workforce narrative-setting (helping employees understand how their roles evolve), skill development programs that run ahead of deployment timelines, manager capability building (frontline managers are the key adoption lever), and transparent communication about which roles are affected and how.

Move 5: Establish an AI Governance Framework That Enables Speed, Not Just Controls Risk

Many companies have built AI governance frameworks that function primarily as brakes. The best frameworks function as both brakes and accelerators—providing rapid-approval pathways for low-risk use cases while maintaining rigorous review for high-risk ones.

The key design principle is tiered governance: an expedited track for low-stakes, reversible deployments; a standard track for customer-facing or medium-consequence applications; and a rigorous track for high-consequence, high-risk uses. Without this tiering, governance becomes a bottleneck that drives valuable experiments underground, outside any oversight at all.

The Competitive Clock Is Running

The companies we work with that are furthest ahead in enterprise AI adoption have been building systematically for 18–36 months. They are not winning because they have better models—those are largely commoditized. They are winning because they have better data, better operating models, better trained workforces, and better governance infrastructure.

That compounding advantage will be very difficult to close for late movers. The window to act is open, but it is not indefinitely so.
